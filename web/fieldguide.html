      Glossary

      The words we use every day and nobody explains on day one. If you're new, read this once and most of the rest of the manual opens up.

      Framing words

      TrackThe U-shaped channel that runs along the floor and the ceiling. Studs sit in it.
      StudThe vertical member. In steel it's a C-shape with an open side.
      On center (o.c.)Measured from the middle of one stud to the middle of the next. "16 o.c." means every 16 inches.
      Gauge / milsHow thick the steel is. Lower gauge number = thicker steel = stronger. Mils is the newer, more precise way of saying the same thing.
      Web / flangeThe web is the flat back of the stud; the flanges are the two sides that stick out. Board screws into the flange.
      Knockout / punchoutThe oval holes stamped in the stud web. Pipe, wire, and bridging go through them.
      LayoutMarking on the floor and deck exactly where every wall goes, before anything is built.
      KeelThe waxy marking crayon used to write on concrete and steel.
      Head of wallWhere the top of the wall meets the structure above. The most detail-sensitive spot in the whole wall.
      DeflectionThe structure above moving down and up as it's loaded. The wall has to allow it.
      Rated wallA wall built to a tested fire-resistance assembly. Everything about it is specified and it gets inspected.
      BackingExtra steel or wood in the wall so something can be screwed to it later.
      Rough opening (RO)The hole framed for a door or window — always bigger than the door itself.

      Drywall words

      Board / rockGypsum wallboard. Same thing.
      HangPutting the board up.
      Tapered edgeThe long edges of a sheet are slightly thinner, forming a shallow recess for tape and mud.
      Butt jointWhere two cut ends meet. No recess, so it's much harder to hide than a tapered seam.
      MudJoint compound.
      Bed / tape coatThe first coat, which embeds the tape.
      SkimA thin coat over the whole surface, not just the joints.
      FeatherThinning the mud out to nothing at the edges so there's no detectable line.
      ScrapeShaving high spots off with a knife instead of sanding them off later.
      Raking lightLight hitting the wall at a low angle. It reveals every flaw — it's how the work gets judged.
      TelegraphWhen something underneath shows through the finished surface.

      Ceiling words

      ACT / CTAcoustical ceiling tile — the lay-in tile ceiling in a grid.
      GridThe metal framework the tiles sit in.
      Wall angleThe L-shaped trim around the room perimeter that the grid lands on.
      Border tileThe cut tiles at the edges of the room. Making these big and equal is the whole art of ceiling layout.
      PlenumThe space above the ceiling.

      Jobsite words

      GCGeneral contractor — who we work for on most jobs.
      SubmittalThe product data we send for approval before installing. Once approved, it governs.
      SpecThe written project specification. It beats habit, always.
      Punch listThe final list of items to fix before the job is accepted.
      T&MTime and material — work billed by hours and materials instead of a fixed price. Document everything.
      Change order (CO)Extra work outside the original contract, priced and approved separately. If it's not on a CO, we're doing it for free.
      Job CheckPortal tool that writes trade stage status to Monday. Only mark what is actually done.
      Job Start / handoffThe Sales→Ops packet Ops accepted before we mobilize. Exclusions and open questions live there.
      Morning BriefPrivate prep on your phone for the day. Not a Slack #operations broadcast.
      Ops lead / superintendentOwns multi-job routing and production calls. Scope and money escalate to the office.
      Pre-board walkThe check before hanging: backing, ROs, MEP, rated layers.
      QC walkShort lead inspection under raking light before a stage is marked done.
      Window returnsHow we finish the drywall (or not) at a window opening — from Job Start, not habit.
      Level 5GA-214 full skim over the whole surface. Not a free upgrade from Level 4.
      Resilient channel (RC)Channel that decouples board from studs for sound. Screws into the stud through it kill the point.
      RFIRequest for information — the written question when documents conflict.
      ExclusionsWhat we did NOT sell — required on Job Start. Asking us to do it is a change order path.
      Open questionsClarifications from the handoff / scope review that must be answered before that work starts.
      Board countSheet count on the handoff and Projects billing basis. Field verifies; does not rewrite money columns.
      Garage finishJob Start status for how far the garage is finished — classic rework when guessed.

      Ladders, scaffold, lifts

      Full detail · dust & silica on our work

      Everyday silica — not just demo

      OSHA's silica rule is not only for knocking out concrete. Cutting board, grinding compound, and power sanding generate respirable dust. Table 1 gives task-based controls (continuous water or vacuum collection for many grinding tasks). Implementing the listed control for that tool is how you stay in compliance without inventing your own plan.

      • Prefer vacuum sanding and shrouded tools over open dry sanding in occupied or multi-trade spaces.
      • Do not "dust out" a room with a leaf blower — that re-floats fines for everyone.
      • Respirator fit testing and a written program belong to the office. Field improvisation ("I brought an N95 from the truck") is not a substitute when the task requires a respirator.

      Deeper demo / ACM / lead stops live on Demolition. This page is the everyday version.

      Hard stops — put the tools down

      Pre-1981 material without a survey (presume PACM). Pre-1978 housing / child-occupied lead rules. Anything structural you were not told to cut. Untraceable conduit or live unknowns. Stop → call the lead → do not resume until someone with authority says so in writing or by clear verbal from the lead/super. Recognizing that a decision is required is the skill — not deciding asbestos yourself.

      Where this came from

      Orientation only — not compliance advice and not a substitute for GVC's written safety programs. OSHA 1926.1101 (asbestos), EPA lead RRP concepts, and OSHA 1926.1153 Table 1 (silica) are cited so you know the names; the office owns the programs behind them. See also Demolition expert block on regs.

      Reading Drawings

      A drawing set isn't a pile of paper — it's an organized system. Learn the organization once and you can find anything in any set, on any job, for the rest of your career.

      In plain words

      Every sheet has a letter and a number. The letter says whose drawing it is — A is the architect's, S is the structural engineer's, M P E are mechanical, plumbing and electrical. Find the title block in the corner for the sheet name, the scale, and the revision. Find the grid lines, because that's what everything gets measured from. And the one rule that will save you more than any other: use the written dimension. Never hold a ruler up to a drawing.

      The two rules that matter most

      1. Never scale off a drawing. Sets get printed, reduced, emailed, and photographed. A sheet that says 1/4" scale may not be at 1/4" anymore, and the error doesn't announce itself. If there's no written dimension for what you need, that's a question, not a measurement.

      2. Check the revision before you build. The title block has a revision number and date. Building accurately off a superseded sheet is still building it wrong, and it's the most expensive mistake on this page.

      How the set is organized

      Sheet numbers are three parts: the discipline letter, the sheet type, and a sequence number. A2.03 is architectural, second series, third sheet.

      AArchitecturalOur home. Floor plans, partition types, reflected ceiling plans, elevations, details, schedules.
      SStructuralDeflection details, tall-wall and bracing requirements, what we can and can't attach to.

      • Floor plans. Where the walls go, and the dimension strings that locate them.

      Finding your way around a sheet

      Full detail · what beats what

      The document hierarchy — and the one thing you must not do

      When two documents disagree, there's an order, and knowing it keeps you out of an argument you can't win later.

      • Change orders and addenda beat the original drawings. They're issued after and they're contractual.
      • A later revision beats an earlier one. Check the revision block.
      • Approved submittals govern the product that actually gets installed.
      • The manufacturer's installation instructions govern installation — and they override general practice, including this manual.
      • Specifications vs. drawings is genuinely project-specific. Most spec books contain their own precedence clause saying which wins. Read that clause rather than assuming; the common pattern is that the spec governs quality and materials while the drawings govern location and quantity, but do not bet a job on that.

      The thing you must not do: pick one and build. A conflict between two contract documents is an RFI, not a field decision. Write the question, send it, and keep working somewhere else while you wait. If you guess and you're wrong, the fix is on us — and the fact that the documents contradicted each other stops being a defense the moment we chose without asking.

      Writing an RFI that actually gets answered

      A good RFI names the sheets and details in conflict, states what you understand each one to require, says what it's holding up, and proposes an answer. "Detail 5/A8.02 shows a slotted deflection track at the corridor head of wall; the partition schedule for type A3 calls for a double slip track. Please confirm which governs.       Rated assemblies · firestopping

      Firestopping

      A rated wall is only rated if every hole through it and every joint around it is sealed with a tested system. Not caulk — a system, with a number.

      In plain words

      When you put a hole in a fire wall, or when there's a gap at the top of one, you can't just fill it with whatever's on the truck. There's a tested assembly with a number — it specifies the exact material, how deep, how big the gap is allowed to be, and what backing goes behind it. Build the number that's on the drawings. And photograph it before it gets covered, because afterward there's no way to prove it was right.

      Why this one is different

      Most of our work is judged on how it looks. This is judged on whether a listed system was reproduced exactly, by an inspector, against a document. Close enough is a failure. It's also the item most likely to hold up a certificate of occupancy — which makes it the item most likely to have a GC standing over you.

      Full detail · reading the number

      UL system numbers decode — this is the whole skill

      A system number looks like gibberish and is actually a description. Once you can read it, you can tell at a glance whether the system on the drawing matches the condition in front of you.

      Joint systems — format XX-Y-NNNN, for example HW-D-1000:

      • First pair = joint type. HW = head-of-wall. WW = wall-to-wall. FW = floor-to-wall. FF = floor-to-floor.
      • Middle letter = movement. D = Dynamic — the joint is expected to move and the system is tested to move with it. S = Static — no movement permitted.
      • Number = nominal joint width. 0000–0999 = up to 2". 1000–1999 = over 2" up to 6". 2000–2999 = over 6" up to 12".

      So HW-D-1000 reads as: head-of-wall joint, dynamic, joint width somewhere over 2" and up to 6".

      Through-penetrations use a similar scheme — W-L-1087 and the like, where W indicates a wall assembly and the following letter identifies the class of wall and penetrant. You mostly need to recognize that the number is specific to the penetrant: a system for a metallic pipe is not a system for a bundle of cables, and swapping them voids it.

      The one that catches people: dynamic vs. static

      Our head-of-wall almost always moves. That's the entire point of the deflection track described in Metal Stud Framing. So the firestop at that joint has to be a dynamic system, tested to open and close through a cycle count.

      Put a static system in a moving joint and it does exactly what you'd expect — it works on the day it's inspected and tears the first time the structure loads. Put a dynamic system in and don't build it to the specified joint width, and it isn't the tested system either.

      F rating and T rating

      • F rating — how long the system stops flame passing through. Expressed in hours.
      • T rating — how long the unexposed side stays cool, specifically under 325°F above ambient. Also in hours, and it can be lower than the F rating on the same system.
      • When T matters: many jurisdictions and specs require T equal to F on walls separating dwelling units or patient rooms, and anywhere combustible material sits against the unexposed side. A system with a 1-hour F and a 0-hour T is perfectly legitimate and completely wrong for those locations.
      • If the spec calls out a T rating and the system you grabbed doesn't have one, that's the wrong system even though the F rating matches.

      Installing to the listing

      • Annular space — the gap between the penetrant and the edge of the hole. The listing gives a minimum and a maximum. Too tight and the system doesn't apply; too generous and it doesn't either. This is the number inspectors measure.
      • Packing / backing material — usually mineral wool, at a specified depth and compression. It's not filler, it's a tested component.
      • Fill depth — the sealant depth is specified. Skinning the surface and leaving a void behind it is the classic field shortcut and it's visible when probed.
      • Sealant type — intumescent (expands with heat, for combustible or melting penetrants like plastic pipe and cable) versus elastomeric (stays flexible, for metallic penetrants and moving joints). Not interchangeable.
      • Substrate clean and dry. Dust and mud on the board face is a bond failure.
      • Both sides where the listing says both sides.
      • Don't overcut the hole. The cheapest way to be unable to install the specified system is a sloppy cutout — which is exactly why hole accuracy is on the Hanging Drywall checklist.

      Labeling: many specs require a permanent label at each firestop giving the system number, installer, and date. If the spec asks for it, it's not optional and it's much easier to do as you go.

      Ranked: what fails inspection

      1. A static system in a dynamic joint — the head-of-wall classic.
      2. Annular space out of the listed range, usually because the hole was cut too big.
      3. Underfilled depth — surface skinned, void behind.
      4. Wrong sealant class — elastomeric where intumescent was required, or vice versa.
      5. Missing packing material, or packed to the wrong depth.
      6. No T rating where the spec required one.
      7. Sealed one side where the listing requires both.
      8. Missing labels where the spec requires them.
      9. No photo record — not a code failure, but it's why we end up re-opening finished work to prove something.

      Before you cover it

      Where this came from

The specific listed system named on the project drawings governs everything here — annular spaces, depths, materials, and cycle counts are properties of that individual listing, not general rules. Scope ownership of penetration firestopping is contractual and varies by project, which is why this document tells you to get it in writing rather than assuming. See Sources.

      Rated assemblies · fire walls & shaftwall

      Fire Walls & Shaftwall

      Everyone calls anything rated a "fire wall." The code doesn't — there are several different things with different rules, and the difference decides how far up the wall has to go.

      In plain words

      A rated wall only works if it's built exactly as the tested assembly says — board type, layers, screws, spacing, joints, and the top of the wall. And the one that catches people: how high it has to go depends on which kind of rated wall it is. Some stop at a rated ceiling, most go all the way to the deck. Read the life safety plan; don't guess from the fact that it's "a fire wall."

      Shaftwall is a different animal. You can't get inside an elevator shaft to build a wall, so shaftwall is designed to be built entirely from the outside — a special J-track, special studs, and a 1" liner panel that slides in from your side.

      Full detail · which rated wall is it

      Shaftwall — built entirely from one side

      Elevator shafts, stairwells, mechanical and trash shafts. You can't work inside the shaft, so the system is designed so you never have to.

      Full detail · shaftwall sequence

      The order, and why it's counterintuitive

      You don't frame the wall and then board it. You build it one bay at a time, alternating liner and stud, because each stud has to capture the liner panel you just placed.

      1. Set the J-runner top and bottom, long flange toward the shaft.
      2. Set the first C-H stud at the end of the run, into the J-track.
      3. Slide a 24" liner panel into the J-runner and into that stud's slot, from your side.
      4. Set the next C-H stud so it captures the free edge of that liner.
      5. Repeat — liner, stud, liner, stud — down the length of the wall.
      6. Then the face board on the room side, joints offset per the listing.

      The liner is 24" wide, so the studs land at 24" o.c. by the system — that's not a spacing choice you get to make, and it's a common source of confusion when someone tries to apply normal partition layout to a shaft.

      What goes wrong on shaftwall

      • Short-cut liner panels. A liner cut sloppy leaves a gap in the rated barrier on the side you can never get back to. Cut accurately, first time.
      • Liner not continuous full height. End joints only where the listing permits, backed where it requires.
      • Screwing the liner to the studs when the system is designed to hold it by friction and tab. Only fasten if the listing says to.
      • Wrong J-runner orientation — long flange must face the shaft, or there's nothing supporting the liner.
      • Missing the deflection head condition. A shaft wall runs to structure and the head still has to accommodate movement.
      • Penetrations. Shaftwall penetrations are especially fussy and there's no second side to correct from.
      • Elevator coordination. Rail bracket attachments, shaft venting, and hoistway requirements all interact with our wall. Talk to the elevator contractor before closing the shaft, not after.
      Fall protection — the real hazard on this scope

      Shaftwall gets installed early, at an open shaft, often many floors up. This is the most dangerous work in this manual by a wide margin. Guarding or fall protection at the shaft opening is not optional and is not something to improvise, and it's the GC's coordination as much as ours. If the opening isn't protected, that's a stop-work condition, not an inconvenience.

      Before you close it up

      Where this came from — read this one

      The fire wall / fire barrier / fire partition / smoke barrier distinctions are code concepts, and this page describes them without giving hour ratings or continuity rules on purpose. Use the project documents. Fall protection at open shafts is governed by OSHA and by the GC's site plan.

      Full detail · head of wall

      Deflection track — the four ways to do it

      Every non-loadbearing partition that runs to structure has to let that structure move without the wall taking the load. The structural drawings say how much movement; the detail says which system. Never carry one job's answer to the next.

      SystemHow it worksPick it when
      Slotted deflection trackVertical slots in the track legs. The stud is screwed through the slot, and the fastener slides in the slot as the structure moves. Common products accommodate up to about 1½" of vertical movement.You want the studs positively attached for lateral load while still allowing vertical movement. Fast, one piece, hard to install wrong.
      Double / slip trackTwo tracks nested. The outer is fixed to the structure; the inner is attached to the studs. The inner assembly slides inside the outer.No special product available, or the detail calls for it.       Deflection clipsA bracket with a slotted leg connects each stud to the structure, used with standard track or in place of deflection track.Higher loads, in-fill curtain wall, or where the track alone can't do the job. Also used in combination with track.
      2D drift track / drift clipsSlots in the legs and the web, so the connection allows vertical deflection and horizontal in-plane drift.Seismic designs that call for drift accommodation. Vertical-only products do not cover drift.

      The rules that get broken:

      • Cut studs short of the track web by the gap the detail requires. A stud bearing hard on the track defeats the whole system and is the most common failed head-of-wall inspection.
      • Never screw through the legs of a plain slip track into the stud. That pins it solid. On slotted track you screw through the slot — that's different and it's correct.
      • Deflection and drift are different problems. Vertical movement is deflection; sideways is drift. A vertical-only product doesn't handle drift.
      • On rated walls the head of wall is a listed joint system — track type, gap, fill material, and fastener pattern are all part of the listing. Build it as listed or it fails.
      Full detail · bracing the studs

      Bridging and bracing — what it's actually for

      An unbraced steel stud doesn't fail by snapping — it twists and buckles sideways about its weak axis. Bridging is the horizontal restraint that stops that. It's a completely different job from diagonal shear bracing, and mixing the two up is a common mistake.

      The point almost everyone misses: bridging only works if it's anchored. The bracing force accumulates along the run, and it has to be delivered into something — a wall end, a corner, a strut, or a designed anchorage point. A run of channel threaded through fifty studs and attached to nothing at the ends is decoration.

      Spacing comes from the design. For reference, the maximum spacing for mechanical bracing of curtain wall framing is commonly cited at 6 feet on center, and for code-prescribed horizontal straps: mid-height on an 8' wall, third points on 9' and 10' walls. Your project detail governs.

      Cold-rolled channel (CRC)

      Solid steel channel, threaded horizontally through the stud knockouts and attached to each stud web — usually with a clip angle. Commonly stocked in 3/4" and 1-1/2" depths.

      • Why it's the workhorse: it sits inside the wall cavity, so it doesn't add thickness and doesn't interfere with the board.
      • Line up the knockouts during stud fill or you'll be fighting every stud to get the channel through. This is why knockout alignment is on the stud-fill checklist.
      • Attachment to each stud is what makes it work — a channel floating loose in the holes braces nothing.
      • Anchor the ends. See above — this is the failure mode.

      Flat strapping, strap and block, and diagonal bracing

      Flat strap is flat steel banding run horizontally across the stud flanges and screwed to each one. Code-prescribed horizontal straps are commonly not less than 1½" wide and 33 mils thick, with one #8 screw per stud flange. Straps normally go on both sides of the wall — a strap on one face alone can still let the stud roll.

      Strap and block adds a short piece of stud or track blocked between studs at intervals along the strap run. This is the method to reach for once studs get deeper than about 6" — U-channel bridging alone doesn't adequately brace members that wide. The block gives the strap something solid to work against and delivers the accumulated force.

      Diagonal strap bracing — X-bracing, sometimes called Z bracing on drawings — is a different animal. It runs on a diagonal across the wall frame to resist racking and lateral load, not stud rotation. It's a shear element. If a drawing shows diagonal straps, they are not a substitute for horizontal bridging, and horizontal bridging is not a substitute for them. Read which one is called for and install both if both are shown.

      Blocking — two different things with the same name

      TypeWhat it doesWho decides it
      Backing blockingGives something a place to be screwed to later — cabinets, grab bars, TVs, handrails, fixtures. Structurally it only has to hold what's hung on it.The backing list, from casework and equipment shop drawings. Our job to catch.
      Structural blockingBraces the studs against rotation and buckling, usually as part of a strap-and-block system. Part of how the wall carries load.The structural detail. Not optional and not a field call.

      They get confused because they look identical in the wall. If someone asks "do we need blocking here," the first question back is "for hanging something, or for bracing?" — the answers come from completely different places.

      Z-furring channel

      Z-shaped channel used to fur out concrete or masonry walls — it holds rigid insulation tight against the substrate and gives you a surface to screw drywall to, all in one member.

      • Depths: commonly 1", 1-1/2", 2", 2-1/2", 3". Stock length is usually 10'.
      • Orientation: installed vertically, with the narrow (about ¾") flange against the wall.
      • Spacing: typically 16" o.c. with gypsum board; can go out toward 24" with thicker, more rigid facing material. Follow the detail.
      • Anchoring: masonry anchors or concrete screws roughly every 16"–24" vertically.
      • Expansion gap: leave about ¼" at floor and ceiling.
      • Insulation fits between the channels; the Z shape traps it against the substrate.
      • Board can run parallel or perpendicular to the channels.

      Where it goes wrong: shimming for an out-of-plane masonry wall. The channel follows whatever it's screwed to, so a wavy block wall gives you a wavy drywall wall. Check the substrate with a string line first and shim the channels, or the finish will show it.

      Full detail · tall walls

      Getting height — the four levers

      How tall a stud can go isn't a judgment call; it comes off a limiting height table published by the manufacturer for that exact stud. There are four ways to buy more height, and they can be combined:

      1. Deeper stud — go from 3-5/8" to 6" to 8". The single biggest lever.
      2. Heavier gauge — thicker steel, lower gauge number. 25ga → 20ga → 18ga → 16ga → 14ga.
      3. Tighter spacing — 24" o.c. → 16" o.c. → 12" o.c.
      4. More bracing — bridging at closer intervals shortens the unbraced length and raises the allowable height.

      Flange width matters too — a wider flange stiffens the stud. To give a sense of the range involved: published tables show a 6" 25ga stud at 16" o.c. with single-layer board both sides reaching roughly 20', the same stud at 24" o.c. dropping to around 15', 20ga reaching into the 30s, and 8" 14ga going to around 40'. These are illustrative of one manufacturer's table — always pull the actual table for the actual product.

      The deflection criterion drives everything

      Every limiting height is tied to a deflection limit — how much the wall is allowed to bow under load. Gypsum board is normally designed to L/240. More brittle finishes — plaster, tile, stucco, cement board — call for L/360 or tighter. The tighter the limit, the stiffer the wall has to be, which means deeper, heavier, closer-spaced, or more braced.

      This is the number to check before pricing a tall wall. The same 20' wall priced at L/240 and built at L/360 is a different wall and a different cost. If the finish schedule shows tile on a tall wall, go find the deflection criterion before the bid goes out.

      Stacked walls vs. one continuous stud

      Two ways to reach a tall height:

      • One continuous stud full height. Simplest structurally and usually preferred — but it's limited by what length you can get, handle, and stand in the space.
      • Stacked walls — build a wall, then build another on top of it, usually landing on an intermediate structure line or a designed horizontal member. Common in atriums, high corridors, and anywhere the material length runs out.

      Splicing studs is not a field decision. If a stud has to be spliced, the lap length, nesting arrangement, and screw pattern come from the engineer. A field-invented splice is a hinge in the middle of a tall wall.

      Stacked walls generally need a real horizontal element at the joint to transfer load, and the deflection detail may repeat at each structure line the wall passes. Anything above the published limiting height table is engineered work — that's the engineer of record's call, and it should be a written answer, not a phone call nobody wrote down.

      Ranked: what actually kills the number

      1. Bad or missing layout — every hour downstream turns into rework.
      2. Walking for material that should have been staged at the wall.
      3. Tool switching — building wall-by-wall instead of by operation.
      4. Coming back for backing after the wall is closed.
      5. Framing ahead of MEP or door frame coordination, then opening walls back up.
      6. Measuring each stud instead of using a story pole.
      7. Nothing written on the slab — the crew re-derives the plan at every wall.
      8. Bridging installed but never anchored — found at inspection, fixed at the worst time.

      Crew and tools

      • One layout hand — your most experienced, running one to two days ahead.
      • Two-man setting teams — one sets and holds, one fastens. Same roles all day.
      • One dedicated stocker/cutter ahead of the teams. Cheaper than four framers walking.
      • Multi-line plumb laser — the single best tool spend in framing. Layout and deck transfer in one setup.
      • Strip-magazine powder tool for bottom track — the reload is the bottleneck.
      • Metal shears over snips — faster, cleaner, no sparks, no hot work permit.
      • Story pole, keel, and a chalk box that holds a line. Unglamorous, and they're in every step above.

      Stocking Any Material

      Stocking looks like the cheapest task on the job, so it gets the least planning. That's exactly why it quietly sets the labor cost of every stage after it.

      In plain words

      Put the material in the room where it gets used. Not by the door, not in one big pile. Every time somebody has to pick a thing up and move it again, that's time nobody paid for.

      Why we work this way

      Material handled twice is pure loss — it produces nothing, it damages material, and it's where people get hurt. The goal of a stocking plan is that a crew member never carries anything further than the room it gets installed in.

      Before the truck is scheduled

      Timing

      • Too early is the more expensive mistake. Material sitting on a job gets damaged, gets stolen, blocks other trades, and gets handled three extra times.
      • Too late stops a crew cold, and an idle crew is the most expensive thing on a jobsite.
      • Target: just far enough ahead that the crew never waits, and no further. Days, not weeks.
      • Stage in waves on long jobs — floor by floor — instead of one giant drop.

      Placement and receiving

      Why the photo matters

      A dated photo of material staged and undamaged settles two arguments that both cost real money: whether the supplier shorted us, and whether another trade damaged our material after we set it. Neither is winnable from memory.

      Closing out

      • Track the overage. Returnable material is real money — it only comes back if someone counts it and calls it in before the return window closes.
      • Consolidate scrap early. Scrap collected as you go costs a fraction of scrap collected at the end.
      • Report actual vs. ordered back to the estimator. That's the only way our waste factors get more accurate.

      General construction material-handling practice plus GVC field experience.       Pre-production · drywall

      Stocking Drywall

      Board is heavy, fragile, awkward, and moisture-sensitive all at once. Nothing else we handle punishes a bad plan this hard — in damaged material and in backs.

      In plain words

      Board goes in the room where it gets hung, stacked in the order it gets hung. Flat, off the floor, out of the weather. On a good job the hanger's first move of the day is picking up a sheet, not looking for one.

      Get it to the floor, not to the ground

      The single biggest decision in drywall stocking, and it's made when the delivery is ordered.

      • Boom truck / crane delivery places board directly on the working floor or through a window opening, several stories up. Default for anything above grade — it does the heavy lifting and it's a safer lift than people on stairs.
      • Telehandler or forklift where the site and openings allow.
      • Window stocking — board through a designated opening before glazing. Needs coordination well in advance; the window open today may be glazed next week.
      • Hand-carrying board up stairs is the last resort, not the plan. If it's the plan, the labor number is wrong.
      Floor loading — ask, don't assume

      A stack of board is one of the heaviest concentrated loads that will ever sit on a suspended floor. Confirm the allowable load with the GC before staging, and spread stacks out rather than piling one large stack in a single spot. Keep stacks away from the middle of long unsupported spans. If nobody can tell you the number, that's your answer — split the load.

      How to stack it

      Distribute by room, off the takeoff

      • Split per room or zone from the takeoff, not one pile per floor.
      • Separate the specialty board clearly — moisture-resistant, abuse-resistant, shaftwall, and rated-assembly board are not interchangeable. A hanger reaching into the wrong stack causes a failed inspection or a tear-out.
      • Label the specialty stacks. A marker on the top sheet takes five seconds.
      • Include a working overage per room so nobody hunts one sheet at the end.
      Injury — the real cost center

      Board handling is where our people get hurt: backs, hands, and crush injuries when a stack shifts. Use panel carts and panel carriers, two-person lifts on full sheets, gloves, and a stable stack that can't tip. A single lost-time back injury costs more than the equipment for a whole year — and costs the person a great deal more than that.

      Before you call it stocked

      Where this came from

      Manufacturer storage-and-handling guidance (keep board flat, dry, off the ground, supported) is consistent across USG, National Gypsum, and CertainTeed. Boom-truck and specialty delivery practice is standard supplier-side material handling. Floor-loading limits are project-specific.

      Job Check · Hanging Status

      Hanging Drywall

      Hanging decides how much finishing there is. Every joint you avoid creating is a joint nobody has to tape, coat three times, sand, and touch up.

      In plain words

      Use the longest sheets that fit, ceilings before walls, top sheet before bottom, and never let a joint land on the corner of a door. Set the screws so they dimple the paper without tearing it. Fewer seams is the whole game — the finishers inherit whatever you leave them.

      Why we work this way

      A hanger's real job is joint reduction. The board goes up either way; what separates a fast job from a slow one is how many linear feet of seam the finishers inherit. A tapered seam is cheap — the factory built a recess for the tape. A butt joint has no recess, so the finisher has to build a wide feathered crown over three coats and it's still the first thing that shows in raking light.

      Before the first sheet

      Sheet layout — where the money is

      • Ceilings before walls. Wall sheets then support the ceiling edge and close the joint.
      • Longest sheet that fits and can be handled safely. Fewer sheets, fewer joints.
      • Perpendicular to the framing where the assembly calls for it — stiffens the wall and puts the tapered edges where you want them.
      • Top sheet first, then bottom on walls, so factory tapered edges meet at the horizontal seam.
      • Stagger butt joints between courses and between adjacent walls. Never let them line up.
      • Keep butt joints out of sight lines — away from the wall a room looks at, away from raking light.
      • Never land a joint on the corner of a door or window opening. That corner is a stress concentration and the crack will find it. Cut so the opening falls in the field of the board.
      • Where a butt joint is unavoidable in a critical area, back-block it to create a slight recess so the finisher has somewhere to put mud.

      Rated assemblies — what's actually locked in

      On a fire-rated wall the board is part of a tested assembly, and every one of these is part of the listing:

      • Board type and thickness
      • Number of layers, and the joint offset between layers — layer two's joints must not land on layer one's
      • Fastener type, length, and spacing — which differs between the base layer and the face layer
      • Joint treatment, including whether joints in the base layer get treated
      • Board orientation
      • Treatment at penetrations, boxes, and the head of wall

      Substituting board, stretching fastener spacing, or lining up joints between layers voids the rating. Build it exactly as listed, and photograph layer one before layer two covers it — that photo is the only evidence that exists afterward.

      Electrical box placement and the required separation between boxes on opposite sides of a rated wall are also part of the listing. Don't move a box to make the board easier.

      Control joints — cracking where you chose

      Large continuous areas of board move, and if you don't give that movement somewhere to go, it shows up as a crack in the middle of a wall. A control joint is a deliberate break in the board with a trim piece over it, so the movement happens at a straight line you picked.

      • Spacing and location come from the project detail and manufacturer guidance — long runs, large ceiling areas, and wherever the structure changes.
      • The board itself must actually be broken at the joint. A control joint bead installed over continuous board does nothing.
      • Framing behind a control joint is usually broken too — a double stud, one on each side.
      • Rated walls need a rated control joint detail; the joint is a gap through the assembly.
      • Also install where a ceiling meets a wall on long runs, and where dissimilar substrates meet.

      Why it matters commercially: cracks in long corridors are one of the most common warranty callbacks in this trade, and nearly all of them trace to missing or ineffective control joints.

      Openings and crew rhythm

      • Cut electrical and mechanical openings accurately and tight — oversized cutouts are a finisher's problem, a fire-caulker's problem, and sometimes a code problem.
      • A router is much faster than measure-and-knife for boxes, if the rough-in is where the drawings say. Measure-and-cut where locations are unreliable.
      • Two-person team: one measuring and cutting, one placing and fastening, with the cutter staying a sheet ahead. Three on volume: one cutting, two hanging.
      • Keep crews intact. People who've worked together for years are substantially faster than the same three newly paired.
      • Ceilings get the lift. Not a luxury — the difference between a productive crew and a wrecked one.

      Scraping

      The cheapest labor in the entire drywall sequence, and it directly replaces the most expensive. This is why it's a tracked stage of its own.

      In plain words

      Before the tapers come in, somebody walks every room with a knife and cleans it up — shave the bumps off the seams, set every proud screw, cut out the boxes that got missed, sweep out the scrap. Then between coats, you shave the ridges off while the mud is still soft. That's it. It's simple work and it saves more time than almost anything else we do.

      Why we work this way

      A knife removes in one second what a sanding pole removes in thirty. Scraping is fast, quiet, dust-free, needs no power, and barely touches the schedule. Sanding is slow, filthy, wrecks the air in the building, requires cleanup, and gets charged at a finisher's rate. Anything you can take off with a blade should never reach a sanding sponge.

      Two scrapes, two different jobs

      PassWhenWhat it removes
      Scrap-out / prepAfter hanging, before tapeTorn paper, board crumbs, proud screws, missed cutouts, loose debris — everything that would make a taper stop and fix something.
      Between-coat scrapeAfter each coat, before the nextRidges, tool lines, lumps, and squeeze-out from the previous coat, while it's still soft enough to shave.

      Pass one — the scrap-out

      This is the handoff pass. The taper should be able to start the moment they walk in.

      Pass two — between coats

      • Timing is the whole trick. Scrape ridges before they harden. Soft compound shaves off with almost no effort; cured compound has to be sanded or chipped.
      • Wide knife held flat — you're shaving high spots, not scraping the joint back open.
      • Don't dig. A gouge creates a low spot that costs another coat to fill. Light pressure, flat blade.
      • Corners and angles collect the worst ridges — the fastest wins are there.
      • Scrape the walls, the ceilings, and the floor. Dropped mud that cures on the slab becomes somebody's chisel work.
      • Squeeze-out at screw heads and butt joints is the classic target — one pass, seconds per wall.
      The trade-off that pays for this stage

      Clean thin coats plus a fast scrape between them reduces sanding to blending edges rather than reshaping the wall. Sanding is the slowest, dirtiest, and most disliked task in the sequence — and dust control, containment, and cleanup all scale with how much of it there is. Everything that reduces sanding pays twice.

      Tools

      • A 6" knife does most of the work. Keep the edge true — a nicked blade leaves lines instead of removing them.
      • A wide knife (10–12") for shaving joint crowns flat without digging.
      • A pole scraper for ceilings and high walls — keeps people off stilts for a task that doesn't need them.
      • A carbide scraper for cured compound that got away from you. This is the tool for when you missed the window.
      • A sharp utility knife for torn paper and missed cutouts.

      What "scraped" means before you flip the status

      Where this came from

      "Scrape ridges before they harden" and "smooth squeeze-out with a knife before sanding" are standard finisher practice and appear in manufacturer sanding guidance, including National Gypsum's. The scrap-out-before-tape pass is GVC's own workflow — already a tracked stage in Job Check between Hanging and Taped. The claim that scraping displaces sanding is a labor-rate argument, not a manufacturer claim; validate the magnitude against our own job costing.

      Finishing Drywall

      Finishing is where the labor is, where the reputation is, and where the two get traded against each other by accident.

      In plain words

      Three coats, each one wider and thinner than the last, each one only over a coat that's fully dry. Scrape between them. Then sand — and if you did the coats right, sanding is just blending the edges, not reshaping the wall. The biggest mistake isn't doing it badly; it's doing it better than the job was priced for.

      Why we work this way

      Hand tools vs. automatic tools

      The biggest equipment decision in the finishing scope.

      • Reported gains are large. Contractors converting from hand finishing to automatic taping and finishing tools commonly report roughly double the crew production, with more conservative reports around 50%. Bazooka operators report at least 40% faster than hand taping, and some report savings on the order of several man-days per week per crew.
      • The catch is real. Full auto-taper rigs are a significant capital cost, they're heavy, and the chains, gears and pumps need constant maintenance. They pay off at high, steady volume — not on scattered small work.
      • The banjo is the middle ground — faster than hand taping, slower than a bazooka, cheap and nearly maintenance-free. Often the right first step.
      • Hand tools stay for small jobs, remodels, patch work, and detail-heavy areas, where setup and cleanup on a rig costs more than it saves.
      • Budget the learning curve. Manual tools are quick to pick up; automatic tools take real practice before the production gain appears. The first jobs will be slower, not faster.
      How to decide it for GVC

      These figures come from tool manufacturers and contractor forums and describe crews at volume. Before buying a rig, run one job with a rented set and measure it against our own historical rate on comparable work. That number is worth more than any published claim.

      Ranked: what actually costs us on finishing

      1. Finishing above the specified level — invisible, unpaid, enormous at scale.
      2. Heavy coats — every extra pound of mud is sanding labor plus drying time.
      3. Coating over damp compound — cracks and delamination that come back after paint.

      Ceilings · CT / ACT

      Acoustical Ceilings

      A ceiling is judged from across the room, all at once, by people with nothing else to look at. Level and layout are the whole job.

      In plain words

      You hang a metal grid from the structure on wires, then drop tiles into it. Two things make it look right: the grid is dead level, and the cut tiles around the edges are big and even. Get those two and it looks professional. Miss either one and everybody notices, even people who don't know why.

      Why layout comes first

      Same principle as framing: the layout carries all the thinking. You work out the tile layout on paper or on the floor before a single wire goes up, because the border tile size is decided the moment you place the first main runner — and it can't be fixed later without taking the grid apart.

      Layout — the part that decides how it looks

      Install sequence

      The scheduling rule that saves the most money

      Grid early, tile late. The grid can go in while other trades are still working above the ceiling — they need it for their layout anyway. Tile is the last thing in the room. Tiling early means damaged, dirty, and lifted tile, and every tile another trade lifts and drops back badly is one you come back for.

      Full detail · the system

      Grid components

      PartWhat it does
      Wall angle / moldingPerimeter trim the grid lands on. Sizes vary; seismic conditions often require a wider (commonly 2") angle.
      Main tee / main runnerThe long load-carrying members, typically at 4' centers, supported by hanger wires.
      Cross teeShorter members clipping between mains to form the module — commonly 2' and 4' lengths for a 2x2 or 2x4 grid.

      Grid face profile — the visible choice

      • 15/16" exposed — the standard wide face. Most common, most economical, most forgiving of small alignment errors.
      • 9/16" narrow — a slimmer, more refined line. Costs more, and it's less forgiving: an out-of-square grid shows more on a narrow face.
      • Slotted grid — required for certain tegular and reveal-edge tiles, where the tile edge engages the grid.
      • Concealed — grid not visible at all. A different system with different access characteristics.

      The grid face and the tile edge are a matched pair. Ordering a tegular tile against a standard grid, or the wrong face width, means the whole ceiling comes back. Confirm both off the same submittal.

      Tile edge types

      EdgeHow it sits
      Square lay-inFlat on top of the grid flange. Simplest and cheapest. The grid reads as a continuous flat plane.
      TegularStepped edge so the tile face drops below the grid, giving a recessed, more finished look. Requires the matching grid profile.
      Reveal / vectorTile sits in a reveal with a shadow line. More architectural, less forgiving of cut quality on borders.

      Border cuts on tegular and reveal tile are skilled work — the cut edge has to be reworked to match the factory edge profile, or the border reads as obviously cut. Price this differently than square lay-in.

      Seismic — what changes and why

      Suspended ceilings are treated as a life-safety item in a seismic event, because a falling ceiling blocks egress and injures people. The requirements come from the project's seismic design category, which comes from the code and the location — never carry the last job's answer to this one.

      In higher categories, expect some combination of:

      • Wider wall angle — commonly 2" — to give the grid room to move without falling off.
      • Two adjacent walls attached, the opposite two free with a required clearance so the grid can move within the perimeter. The grid end typically needs a minimum clearance (commonly 3/4") from the wall and must be free to slide on the closure angle or a perimeter clip.
      • Splay wires and compression posts at intervals to brace the ceiling laterally and hold it down.
      • Independent support for light fixtures and heavy items, with their own wires.
      • Perimeter clips as an alternative to a wide angle in some systems.
      • Clearance around penetrations — sprinkler heads and anything passing through need room to move.

      The one that gets missed: the ceiling has to be able to move. Fastening all four sides tight to the wall angle feels tidy and defeats the entire design.

      Ranked: what actually goes wrong on ceilings

      1. Sliver borders — a layout decision that can't be fixed afterward.
      2. Out of level — visible from across the room and from every angle.
      3. Out of square — every tile in the room shows it.
      4. Wires not wrapped properly, or tied to ductwork instead of structure — an inspection failure and a safety issue.
      5. Fixtures hung off the grid without their own support.
      6. Tile installed before the dirty trades finished — damaged and soiled tile, all of it replaced.
      7. Grid and tile edge mismatched — a submittal error that shows up as a full reorder.
      8. Handling tile without clean gloves — fingerprints don't clean off.

      Crew and rhythm

      • Two-person team: one on the laser and layout, one hanging wire and setting grid.
      • Do all the wall angle in the space first, then all the wire, then all the main tees — the same batching that works everywhere else in this manual.
      • A rotating laser pays for itself on the first ceiling. Level is the whole job and eyeballing it doesn't work.
      • Keep tile boxed and off the floor until the grid is complete and the room is clean.

      Casework · cabinet installation

      Installing Cabinets

      Cabinets are judged up close, every day, by someone opening a door. Level and plumb aren't quality items here — they're the whole job.

      In plain words

      Find the highest spot on the floor. Snap your level lines off that. Mark every stud. Hang the uppers first so you're not reaching over base cabinets. Then set the bases and shim them dead level. Scribe the fillers, then adjust the doors last. If the run is level and plumb, everything after it is easy — and if it isn't, nothing after it will save you.

      Why the high point, and why uppers first

      Floors are never flat. If you measure your line up from a low spot, the cabinets sitting at the high spot end up too tall — they won't fit under the uppers, or the countertop won't sit right. Measuring from the high point means every other cabinet gets shimmed up to the line, which is the direction shims work.

      Uppers before bases because once the bases are in you're reaching over them for every screw, every level check, and every clamp. Hanging uppers in open air with a temporary ledger is dramatically faster than hanging them leaning over a counter run.

      Before you start — the one that ruins a day

      Confirm the backing is in the wall. Cabinets need solid framing to fasten to, and if that blocking was missed at framing, you find out with a cabinet in your hands. This is the same backing list that's on the Metal Stud Framing checklist — casework and countertops are the first two items on it. Check before the truck shows up, not on install day.

      Full detail · fastening

      Screws — this one actually fails

      Use cabinet installation screws, not drywall screws. Cabinet screws have a washer head that won't pull through the hanging rail, a coarse thread for good purchase in framing, and — critically — a ductile shank that bends before it breaks.

      Drywall screws are hardened and brittle. They're designed for shear in a thin panel, not for sustained tension. An upper cabinet loaded with dishes hanging on drywall screws is a known failure mode, and it fails suddenly, all at once, with the contents in it. It is not a corner worth cutting on a job where anyone will ever open that door.

      Length matters too: long enough to get real purchase into the framing through the rail, the drywall, and any shim stack behind the cabinet. Count the shim thickness — a cabinet shimmed out 3/8" needs a longer screw than the same cabinet tight to the wall.

      Into steel studs

      On our own jobs the wall behind is often steel, and cabinet screws for wood don't hold in 25ga steel. That's exactly why the backing matters: the framing detail should have put a solid backing member — heavier-gauge steel, flat strap and block, or a wood member — behind the cabinet line. Fasten to the backing.

      If the backing isn't there and the cabinets are on site, stop and solve it properly. Toggle-type anchors into drywall alone are not an answer for loaded upper cabinets. This is the moment to make a phone call, not a judgment call.

      Shimming and scribing

      • Composite shims over wood where you can — they don't compress over time and they don't split when you drive a screw through them.
      • Shim in pairs, opposed, so you get a parallel bearing surface rather than a wedge that walks out.
      • Score and snap shims flush after fastening. A shim left proud fouls the toe kick and the trim.
      • Scribing a filler: set the piece in place, run a compass or scribe tool along the wall to transfer the wall's shape onto the filler, then cut and dress to the line with a block plane or belt sander. A scribed filler against a wavy wall is what separates a finished job from an obvious one.
      • Leave yourself scribe material. A filler cut to exact size has nothing to remove. Cut it fat and take it down.
      • Tall and pantry cabinets amplify error — a small out-of-plumb at the base becomes a visible lean and a door that swings on its own at the top. Check these twice.

      What the customer actually looks at

      Nobody checks your levels. They open a door, and they look down a run of doors and drawers. Reveal consistency is what reads as quality — the gaps between doors, and between doors and frames, being uniform down the whole run.

      • Adjust hinges for side-to-side, in-and-out, and height — most European hinges adjust all three, and most people only ever use one.
      • Sight down the run at eye level after adjusting. Gaps that measure right can still look wrong if a case is racked.
      • Uneven reveals almost always trace back to a racked case, not the hinge. If a door won't adjust, check whether the cabinet got bowed by over-tightened screws against an out-of-plane wall — the fix is shims, not more hinge adjustment.
      • Drawer fronts get the same treatment, and drawers get checked for smooth full travel and soft-close engagement.
      • Hardware last, off a template or jig so every pull lands identically. A drilled hole is not reversible.

      Ranked: what actually goes wrong

      1. Measuring from the low point of the floor instead of the high point — the whole run ends up wrong and it's discovered at the countertop.
      2. Backing not in the wall — found with a cabinet in your hands.
      3. Drywall screws instead of cabinet screws — a failure that happens later, loaded, with someone standing there.
      4. Not shimming behind the fastening points — bowed backs, racked cases, doors that never line up.
      5. Setting bases before uppers — slow, awkward, and harder to get right.
      6. Not checking the walls and corners before starting.
      7. Appliance openings not verified until the appliance shows up.
      8. Adjusting doors before everything's fastened — doing the same work twice.
      9. Filler problems discovered at the end of a run — cabinets come back off.

      Tools

      • Rotating or self-leveling laser — for finding the floor's high point and carrying level lines around a room. Non-negotiable.
      • Face-frame clamps — the specific clamps that pull two face frames flush. Ordinary clamps mar the finish and don't align faces.
      • Cabinet jacks or a support ledger — either one; the ledger is free.
      • Composite shim assortment.
      • Cabinet installation screws in more than one length.
      • Levels: a long one for the run, a 4' for cases, a torpedo for tight spots.
      • Scribe tool or compass, plus a block plane and a belt sander for dressing to the line.
      • Drill/driver with a clutch — over-driving is how backs get bowed.
      • Hardware jig for pulls and knobs.
      • Blankets or cardboard for the floor and for setting doors down on. Damage on finished casework is expensive and obvious.

      Before construction · selective demolition

      Demolition

      The only procedure in this manual where the first step is a legal question, not a construction one. Get that part wrong and nothing else matters.

      In plain words

      Before anything comes down, find out what's in it. In an older building that means asbestos and lead, and that is not a judgment call — there's a survey and a paper trail, and somebody has to hand it to you. Then protect what's staying, contain the dust, kill the power, and take it apart in the reverse order it went together. If you find something you don't recognize, stop and don't disturb it.

      Hard stops — do not proceed, call the office

      Pre-1981 construction. OSHA presumes that thermal system insulation and surfacing material in buildings constructed no later than 1980 contain asbestos — that's what "PACM" means. Commercial and multi-unit residential buildings scheduled for demolition or major renovation require a formal survey. If nobody can produce a survey, you must presume suspect material contains asbestos until sampling proves otherwise.

      Pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities. EPA's lead Renovation, Repair and Painting rule applies, which requires a certified renovator and specific containment and cleanup practices. Not something to discover halfway through.

      Anything structural. Never remove a wall, post, beam, or bearing element without an engineer's direction in writing. "It's just a partition" is a conclusion, not an observation.

      Any pipe, conduit, or wire you can't positively trace. Assume it's live and in service.

      Disturbing suspect material turns a survey into an abatement, with our name on it. There is no version of this where guessing is cheaper than asking.

      Full detail · who regulates what

      Two agencies, two different concerns, both apply

      • OSHA — 29 CFR 1926.1101 protects the worker. It's the source of the PACM presumption for pre-1981 thermal system insulation and surfacing material, and it defines removal to include demolition operations.
      • EPA — NESHAP protects the air, and governs notification and work practices for structural changes to regulated facilities.
      • They are not alternatives. Complying with one does not satisfy the other.
      • State and local rules are often stricter, with their own notification windows before demolition begins. That's the office's job to confirm, but it's why "we're starting Monday" can become "we're starting in two weeks."

      What this means for the field: you are not the person who decides whether something is asbestos. You are the person who recognizes that a decision is required and stops. That's the whole job on this page.

      Silica — the one that applies to our everyday work too

      OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1153 covers respirable crystalline silica, which comes off cutting, grinding, and breaking concrete and masonry. Its Table 1 pairs common construction tasks with required dust controls and respiratory protection — and an employer who fully implements the controls Table 1 specifies for that task is deemed compliant, which is why working to Table 1 is the practical path. For handheld grinders the control is continuous water to the wheel or vacuum dust collection.

      This isn't only a demo issue. Drywall workers are exposed when cutting board and sanding joints, which is a live reason to use vacuum-assisted sanding — see Finishing Drywall. Whether GVC has a written exposure control plan and fit-tested respirators is a question for the office, not a field improvisation.

      Before the first swing

      Protection and containment

      • Protect what's staying, before you start. Floors, casework, glass, elevator cabs, finished walls at the boundary. Cheaper than repairing, and far cheaper than arguing about who damaged it.
      • Dust containment — poly walls with zipper doors, taped seams, and a real boundary rather than a suggestion.
      • Negative air with HEPA filtration in occupied buildings and anywhere the GC requires it. It also keeps our dust out of other trades' finished work.
      • Mask return air grilles and shut down affected HVAC — otherwise the building's own air system distributes your dust into finished space.
      • Protect egress. Exits stay clear and exit signage stays visible, even mid-demo.
      • Walk the boundary daily and re-tape what's come loose.

      In the wall · insulation

      Insulation

      The fastest trade in this manual to install badly in a way nobody can see. It gets covered the same day, and it either performs or it doesn't.

      In plain words

      Insulation works by trapping still air. That means it has to fill the cavity completely and it must not be squashed. Cut batts to fit snug — a batt jammed in behind a pipe or stuffed short at the top is doing a fraction of its job. Gaps and compression are the only two ways to get this wrong, and they're both invisible an hour later.

      Why care, when nobody will ever see it

      Because the two things it's usually there for are things people complain about for the life of the building: energy bills and sound. A sound wall with a two-inch gap at the top of every cavity is not a sound wall, and the complaint arrives after the building is occupied — when opening the wall costs a hundred times what filling the gap would have.

      Know which one you're installing

      Installing batts

      Full detail · sound walls

      The batt is only part of the STC

      An STC rating belongs to the whole assembly, not the insulation. Board layers, stud type and spacing, whether the wall runs to deck, and — most of all — whether the assembly is sealed all matter as much as the batt. A sound wall leaks like a bucket with a hole.

      • Seal the perimeter. Acoustic sealant at the top and bottom track and at the edges, where the detail calls for it. An unsealed gap at the floor undoes the batt above it.
      • Seal penetrations — boxes, sleeves, conduit. Sound goes through every hole you leave.
      • Back-to-back electrical boxes on opposite sides of a sound wall are an acoustic hole. Offset them where you can, and flag it where you can't.
      • The wall has to go where the sound goes. A sound-rated partition stopped at a suspended ceiling, with an open plenum above it, transmits straight over the top — and no amount of batt in the wall below fixes that. If the drawings show STC and the wall stops at the ceiling, ask the question.
      • Full-height insulation above the ceiling line, on to-deck walls, is not optional detail work — it's the part that makes the rating real.

      Working safely with it

      • Long sleeves, gloves, and eye protection, always. Fiberglass and mineral wool both irritate skin and eyes, and mineral wool is the rougher of the two.
      • Respiratory protection per the manufacturer's guidance and GVC's program — overhead work drops fibers straight into your face.
      • Wash work clothes separately.
      • Keep bags closed until you're at the wall; loose batts fill a room with fiber.

      Finishes · production painting

      Painting

      Paint doesn't hide anything. It reveals. Every hour of prep buys back more than an hour of paint, and no amount of good paint rescues bad preparation.

      In plain words

      Protect everything, clean everything, prime everything bare. Then cut in the edges and roll while the cut-in is still wet so it blends. Two coats means two coats — one heavy coat is not the same thing. And check the sheen on the drawings before you start, because the shinier the paint, the more it shows every flaw underneath it.

      The handoff that decides the whole job

      The paint job is only as good as the drywall under it, and the drywall is only as good as the finish level that was specified. A Level 4 wall under semi-gloss paint in raking light will generate complaints that look like painting defects and aren't. See Finishing Drywall → levels of finish. Catch that mismatch before the first coat, not after.

      Repair · patch work

      Drywall Patch

      Patching is judgment work. Picking the right method for the size of the hole is most of the job.

      In plain words

      Small hole, one method. Big hole, a different one. Then spread the mud out much wider than you think you need to and feather it to nothing. Patches show because the mud stopped too soon, not because the hole was too big.

      Why we work this way

      The patch fails at its edges, not in its middle. Almost every visible patch is visible because the mud was feathered over too small an area, so there's a detectable bump or halo where the repair meets the wall. A wide, thin, well-feathered patch disappears; a small thick one always shows.

      Pick the method by the size of the hole

      The California / butterfly patch

      Uses the board's own paper as the tape, so there's nothing to blister and no backing to install.

      The backed patch, for anything bigger

      Rated walls and smoke barriers

      A hole in a fire-rated wall is a breach of a tested assembly, and a cosmetic patch does not restore it. Repair with the same board type and thickness, same number of layers, same fastener pattern, and the specified joint treatment. Penetrations get the listed firestop system. If you don't know the assembly, stop and find out — this is life safety and it gets inspected.

      Speed without shortcuts

      • Setting compound for the fill coats. It sets chemically instead of drying, so you can put multiple coats on in one visit instead of one coat a day. The single biggest time saver in patch work.
      • Pick a set time you can actually use, and clean tools before it sets.
      • Topping or lightweight for the final coat, even if the fills were hot mud — setting compound is hard to sand and the last coat is the one you sand.
      • Batch the work. Walk the whole area, mark every patch, then do all the cut-outs, then all the fills, then all the coats. Same logic as production framing.
      • Feather wider than feels necessary. This is the difference between an invisible patch and a callback.

      Finishing the patch out

      Where this came from

      California/butterfly patch dimensions (a scrap 3–4" oversize with a 1½–2" paper flange) are the standard published method and consistent across trade sources. Setting-compound behavior and same-day recoating follow manufacturer guidance. Rated-assembly repair requirements come from the listing for the specific assembly.

      Punch · step 1 · drywall finish repair

      Drywall Touch-Up

      Fixing the wall itself, not the paint. Found before the painter it costs minutes. Found after, it costs the repair plus a repainted wall.

      In plain words

      This is fixing the drywall — screw pops, dings, ridges you can see in a seam, fuzzy spots where somebody sanded through the paper. Walk the room with a bright light held flat against the wall; that's how you find them. Fill thin, feather it out wide, match the texture, and prime it before anybody paints over it.

      Why this is its own step, before paint

      A drywall defect found before the painter costs a knife, a little mud, and a dab of primer. The identical defect found after paint costs that same repair, plus priming, plus repainting the entire plane corner to corner — because a spot repaint on a finished wall flashes (that's the whole subject of Paint Touch-Up).

      So the economics of the whole punch phase come down to one thing: find the drywall defects before the paint goes on. One careful raking-light walk before the painter mobilizes is the cheapest hour in the finishing scope.

      Punch · step 2 · paint touch-up

      Paint Touch-Up

      The last thing anyone sees and the easiest thing to get visibly wrong. The failure has a name — flashing — and it has specific, avoidable causes.

      In plain words

      Same paint, same can, same tool, and prime anything bare first. If you rolled it originally, roll the touch-up; if you brushed it, brush it. And if the paint has any shine to it, spot touch-up usually won't work — plan on recoating the whole wall from corner to corner.

      Do the drywall first

      This step assumes the Drywall Touch-Up pass is done and every repair is primed. Painting over an unprimed repair is the number one cause of a touch-up that shows, and no amount of care with the paint will fix it.

      Full detail · the physics

      Why touch-ups flash

      A touch-up that shows is almost never a color problem. It's a sheen and porosity problem. The paint matches; what doesn't match is how the surface reflects light and how much paint it absorbed. So the fix is about surface preparation and application method, not a better color match.

      • Different porosity. A bare or patched spot absorbs paint at a different rate than the wall around it. It drinks the binder in, the sheen drops, and you get a dull spot.
      • Different applicator. A roller leaves stipple; a brush leaves a different texture. Brushing over a rolled wall shows in the light even at identical color.
      • Different batch. Small variations between batches show up as sheen or tone differences across a large flat surface.
      • Different conditions. Temperature, humidity, and dry time all affect how a coating lays down and cures.
      • Sheen level. The higher the sheen, the less forgiving. This is the biggest single factor.

      The rules

      When to stop touching up and recoat

      Spot touch-up works on flat paint in normal light. It fights you on eggshell and satin, and it essentially does not work on semi-gloss and gloss, or on any surface under raking or critical light. In those conditions the right call is to recoat the full plane — corner to corner, break to break — rather than chase spots. Recognizing this early saves an afternoon of making the wall worse. On a job with a lot of sheen or critical lighting, price the recoat rather than assume touch-up.

      Working the paint pass

      The walk-and-mark and all the drywall repairs happened in Drywall Touch-Up. This pass is paint only.

      Keep on the truck

      • Labeled paint from every job — job name, room, product, sheen, color code, date. Worth more than anything else on this list.
      • Roller covers in the naps used on the job, and a clean brush.
      • Fast-setting compound and a small selection of knives.
      • Stain-blocking primer and a general-purpose primer.
      • A bright work light for checking at a low angle.
      • Low-tack tape for marking, and drop cloths — a touch-up that leaves a drip is a net loss.
      Where this came from

      Flashing causes and remedies follow published guidance from paint manufacturers — Sherwin-Williams' material on paint flashing, plus trade-press coverage of why patches telegraph. The rules (prime the patch, same product, same batch, brush-over-brush and roller-over-roller, thin slightly to blend) are consistent across those sources. The "recoat the full plane on sheens above flat" guidance is standard painting-contractor practice and the item most likely to save GVC money on punch work.

      Wall panels · FRP

      FRP Panels

      A plastic panel glued to a wall, which sounds simple and punishes two specific mistakes: a substrate that isn't flat, and not leaving the panel room to move.

      In plain words

      FRP is a tough plastic sheet, glued flat to the wall, with plastic trim covering the joints. It goes in kitchens, restrooms and anywhere that gets hosed down. Two things decide whether it looks right: the wall behind it has to be genuinely flat, because FRP telegraphs every bump underneath it — and you have to leave gaps at the edges, because the panels grow and shrink. Butt them tight and they buckle.

      The two failures, and they're both permanent

      1. An uneven substrate. High and low spots must be leveled before installation. On concrete block or brick — which are almost never flat enough — the accepted approach is furring strips with a suitable substrate over them, not FRP straight onto the masonry. A wavy wall behind FRP reads as a wavy glossy wall, and gloss makes it worse.

      2. No expansion allowance. FRP moves with temperature and humidity. Every manufacturer specifies gaps, and a panel installed tight has nowhere to go but outward.

      Before you glue anything

      Full detail · adhesive, gaps, moldings

      Adhesive

      • 100% coverage of the panel back, applied in a cross-hatch pattern with the trowel the adhesive manufacturer specifies.
      • The adhesive must make contact across the entire substrate. Incomplete contact is what causes bubbles, and a bubble in FRP cannot be fixed — the panel comes off.
      • Roll the panel with a laminate roller to drive out air pockets. Work from the centre outward.
      • Follow the adhesive's open time. Too soon and it slides; too late and it doesn't grab.

      Expansion gaps — the numbers

      For a standard 4' × 8' panel, the typical published requirement is a minimum 1/4" gap at top and bottom of each panel and 1/8" between panels. Gaps are also required around fixtures and anything penetrating the panel. Confirm against the actual manufacturer's instructions — these vary by product and panel size, and they govern.

      Moldings and sealant

      • Trim family: inside corner, outside corner, division bar between panels, and cap at the top edge.
      • Silicone sealant into the channel of the molding — that's what makes the installation moisture resistant rather than just moisture shedding.
      • Fit moldings leaving a minimum 1/8" between the panel edge and the molding stem, so the panel can still move inside the trim.
      • In washdown and high-moisture areas, silicone between the moldings and the floor and ceiling as well — otherwise water finds its way behind the panel, and behind the panel is the one place you can't inspect.

      Cutting

      • Fine-tooth blade, cut with the finished face up to keep the chip-out on the back.
      • Support the panel fully so it doesn't flex and crack at the cut.
      • Cut penetrations slightly generous — the gap gets covered by the escutcheon or sealed, and a tight cut has no expansion room.

      Before you call it done

      Where this came from — read this one

      Substrate flatness, 100% cross-hatch adhesive coverage, laminate rolling, silicone in molding channels, and the 1/4" / 1/8" expansion gaps for a 4×8 panel all come from published FRP manufacturer installation guides (Crane Composites, Glasliner, Marlite and similar).       Openings · doors, frames & hardware

      Doors & Hardware

      The frame decides everything. Set it plumb, square, and the right width, anchored to real framing — and the door hangs and latches. Set it wrong and no amount of hardware adjustment will rescue it.

      In plain words

      Get the frame right and the rest is easy. Both jambs plumb, the head level, the throat the right width, and anchored where the frame is made to be anchored. Keep the spreader on the bottom until it's fastened so the frame can't pinch in. Then hang the door, fit the hardware off the template, and adjust the closer last. If a door won't latch, the problem is almost always the frame, not the lock.

      Why this connects to our framing

      Hollow metal frames in steel stud walls get set while the wall is being framed, and they anchor into the jamb studs we build. That means the rough opening, the jamb stud detail, and the frame anchor locations are all part of the framing pass — see Metal Stud Framing → openings. A frame is only as straight as the studs beside it.

      Setting a hollow metal frame

      Full detail · labeled openings and ADA

      Rated openings — the whole opening is the assembly

      On a fire-rated opening, the door, the frame, the hardware, and the gasketing are all part of one listed assembly. Every component carries a label, and the assembly is only rated if all of them are correct together.

      • Labels must remain legible. Painting over a label, or removing one, fails inspection — a label is the only proof the assembly is what it claims to be.
      • No field modification of a labeled door or frame. Cutting a new hardware prep, drilling a new hole, or trimming a labeled door voids the label. If the prep is wrong, that's a supplier problem, not a field fix.
      • Rated openings generally require a self-closing and positive-latching assembly. A closer that doesn't close it, or a latch that doesn't engage, is a rated-assembly failure even though everything is installed.
      • Undercut is limited on rated doors — you cannot simply trim the bottom to clear new flooring.
      • The rated opening sits in a rated wall, which means the wall around it matters too. See Firestopping.

      Accessibility — code, and it gets checked

      Accessible openings carry requirements that are easy to defeat accidentally during installation:

      • Clear width when the door is open — measured to the door face, not the frame.
      • Maneuvering clearance on both sides, which depends on approach direction and whether the door is push or pull side. This is the one most often lost to a wall built slightly in the wrong place — which is why it's worth catching at framing.
      • Opening force limits on interior non-rated doors — a closer cranked down too tight fails this.
      • Hardware operable without tight grasping, pinching, or twisting of the wrist — lever handles rather than knobs — and mounted within the required height range.
      • Threshold height limits, and smooth-surface requirements at the bottom of the door.
      • Closer timing — minimum closing time from a specified angle.
      • The exact dimensions come from the applicable accessibility standard for the project. Get them from the drawings, not from memory.

      Hardware

      • Work from the hardware schedule / hardware sets. Each set lists every item for that opening.
      • Confirm handing before you prep or install anything. A reversed lever is a reorder.
      • Use the manufacturer's template for every prep. Backset and centerline are not eyeball dimensions.
      • Install order: hinges, then lock or latch, then closer, then stop and silencers. Adjust closers last — closing speed, latch speed, and backcheck.
      • Check the door swings clear of everything through its full arc, including anything another trade installed after the frame went in.

      Acoustic panels · Tectum

      Tectum Panels

      Cementitious wood-fiber acoustic panel. Genuinely easy to install and genuinely easy to wreck — almost every defect is a crushed edge or an over-driven screw.

      In plain words

      Tectum is a stiff panel made of wood shreds held together with cement. It absorbs sound and takes a beating, so it goes in gyms, corridors, pools and mechanical rooms. It screws straight to framing or furring. Two rules do most of the work: screw heads flush with the face — never countersunk, and don't bang the corners, because a crushed corner can't be repaired.

      What it is and where it goes

      • Aspen wood fibers with a cementitious binder — that's why it's abrasive to cut and why it holds a screw well.
      • Chosen for acoustic absorption plus impact resistance, which is why it shows up where a soft acoustic tile would get destroyed.
      • Direct-attach panels go straight to a ceiling or wall structure, unlike lay-in ACT which needs a grid — see Acoustical Ceilings.
      • Standard direct-attach panels are 1" thick, in nominal 2×4', 2×8' and 4×8' sizes. Other thicknesses and product lines exist — check the submittal.
      Full detail · attachment

      Fasteners and spacing

      • Panels can be attached with screws, nails, or adhesive depending on the substrate and the submittal.
      • With screws, the pull-through resistance of the screw head is adequate on its own — no washers and no adhesive are required alongside them.
      • Minimum 24" o.c., starting at the panel edge.
      • When mechanically attached to furring, fasten every 12" o.c.
      • Fastener selection has to account for total system weight — the panel, plus anything the assembly carries. Substrate governs the fastener type.

      The two mistakes

      Do not countersink. Screw heads sit flush with the face of the panel. Driving them in crushes the fiber around the head, leaves a dark dimple, and there is no filler that makes it disappear — the texture is the finish. Set the clutch and test on a scrap first.

      Do not let the substrate telegraph. Tectum is stiff but it won't bridge a wavy wall or an out-of-plane furring run. String the substrate and shim the furring before you start, the same as with Z-furring.

      Cutting and handling

      • Carbide blade — the cementitious binder eats ordinary blades, and a dull blade tears the fiber instead of cutting it.
      • Dusty and abrasive. Eye protection and dust control, and it's a cut worth doing outside the finished space.
      • Cut edges are visible and don't match the factory edge. Plan the layout so cuts land where they're least seen, and check the submittal for whether cut edges get treated or painted.
      • Corners crush. Carry panels on edge, stack them flat, and don't lean them against anything.

      Layout and finish

      • Lay it out like a ceiling: plan the joints, keep border pieces large and even, don't finish on a sliver. Same principle as Acoustical Ceilings.
      • Keep joints tight and aligned — the panel texture is busy, but a wandering joint line still reads.
      • If it's field painted, follow the manufacturer's method. Heavy rolled paint fills the texture and kills the acoustic performance — which is the entire reason the panel is there.

      Before you call it done

      Where this came from — read this one

      "Didn't you get the updated set?"A revision we were never issued. If the wall moved, that's a change even if nobody calls it one. Check your revision against theirs.
      "Can you work around that / fix that for us?"Another trade's mistake or their material in our way. Fixing it is their cost, not ours — but only if it's documented before we touch it.
      "We'll need you to come back for that area."Somebody else's sequence forcing us to mobilize twice. Two trips instead of one is a real cost even though the work itself didn't change.
      "It's supposed to be like that."Existing conditions or a substrate that doesn't match the drawings — an out-of-plane masonry wall, a slab out of tolerance, framing not where it's dimensioned. Extra work to accommodate it is a change.

      The two nobody ever bills

      • Standing by. A crew on site unable to work because another trade isn't finished, the area isn't released, or there's no power, heat, or access. That's paid time producing nothing, and it's recoverable only if somebody wrote down the date, the hours, the crew, and the reason, on the day.
      • Working out of sequence or stacked with other trades. Three trades in one room is slower than one trade in one room, and that inefficiency is real. It's also almost impossible to claim after the fact, which is exactly why the daily record matters.

      If the crew is standing around, that's not a break — that's a documentation event.

      What to do, in the next ten minutes

      Who can actually authorize extra work

      The GC's superintendent or project manager, or the owner's representative, depending on our contract. Another subcontractor can never authorize our extra work, no matter how confidently they ask — and the electrician who asks you to move a wall six inches is not going to be the one paying for it.

      If you're not certain the person asking has authority, that's the clearest possible signal to call the office before doing anything.

      Time and material tickets

      • Filled out the same day, while you remember the hours.
      • Names, hours, and what they did — not "crew, 8 hours."
      • Material actually used, with quantities.
      • Signed by the GC's rep before you leave site. An unsigned ticket found in a truck two weeks later is a negotiation. A signed one is a bill.
      • Photos attached to the ticket, same day.
      • Then it goes to the office and into the Change Order tool — that's how it becomes money instead of a story.

      When it is not a change order

      This matters as much as the rest of the page. Claiming things that aren't changes costs us credibility, and credibility is what gets the real ones approved.

      • Our own rework. A wall we built in the wrong place is on us.
      • Our own damage. If we scratched it, we fix it.
      • Work that's in the set and we missed it at bid. Painful, but it's ours. That's an estimating lesson, not a change order.
      • Normal coordination. Some back-and-forth with other trades is just the job.
      • Cleanup of our own scrap. Always ours.

      The honest version: a change order is for work that genuinely wasn't in what we agreed to do. Nothing more, nothing less.

      Ranked: what this costs us when it goes wrong

      1. Verbal adds performed and never billed — the single largest silent margin loss on most jobs.
      2. Standing-by time never recorded — paid hours, zero recovery.
      3. Another trade's damage repaired before photographing it — becomes our cost the moment we touch it.
      4. Building to a revision we were never issued, then eating the rework.
      5. Unsigned T&M tickets — turns a bill into a negotiation.
      6. Proceeding on a verbal from someone without authority.
      7. Finding out weeks later, when nobody remembers and there are no photos.
      8. Claiming things that aren't changes — spends the credibility we need for the real ones.
      Where this came from

      This is GVC practice and standard subcontract administration, not a technical standard. What counts as extra work is decided by our contract on each individual job — the scope, the drawings and revisions listed in it, and its notice provisions. Many subcontracts contain a written-notice deadline for claiming extra work, measured in days from when the condition was discovered; missing that window can forfeit the claim no matter how legitimate it is. That's the strongest possible reason to report the same day rather than at the end of the month. Andrea and the office own the contractual side; the field's job is to notice, stop, document, and call.

      Why this manual has no production rates in it

      The published figures range widely — roughly 35–40 sheets per man-day in some sources, 40 sheets per two-man crew in others, 2,500–3,500 SF of ceiling per day for a two-person crew, 4,000–5,000 SF for walls. These disagree by a factor of two or more, because they describe different work, conditions, and crews. Publishing any of them as a GVC standard would give our estimators and PMs a number that feels authoritative and isn't. GVC's own job history is the only source worth planning against — and now that Job Check stamps stage completions per project, we can start building it.
